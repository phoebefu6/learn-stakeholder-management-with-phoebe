Coalitions and pre-wiring

A proposal that walks into the executive meeting cold gets debated, and debate is where good ideas go to be postponed. A proposal that walks in pre-wired gets ratified, because every person in the room has already seen it, shaped it and traded something for it. Tonight we put Sunniva's one-to-ones in order, work out what each person actually trades in, and draw the line between influence and manipulation sharply enough to use on a Monday.

Part 1 · covers sequencing the one-to-ones

Nobody hears it first in the room

5 min live

Pre-wiring is common practice in organisations that get things done: before a proposal reaches the meeting that decides it, every person who matters has seen it in a one-to-one, said what worries them, and seen the proposal change because of it. The meeting then ratifies a decision the room has already half-made. The work is in the order: from the person asked first, outwards, to the signer last.

LiveThe meeting that decides should ratify, not debate3 min▶

An executive meeting is the worst place to hear something new. People react in front of their peers, a doubt voiced in public has to be defended, and the easiest safe answer is "let's take this offline", which is a polite no for a quarter. The same doubt raised in a one-to-one costs nobody face and can be fixed before anyone else hears it.

So the norm Sunniva works to is simple: nobody hears the proposal for the first time in the meeting where it is decided. By the time Fernhollow's executive committee meets, Halvard has seen the cost by year, Odile has seen her depot hours capped, Rafe has seen his CRM stay his, and each of them knows the others have been asked. The meeting confirms what is already true.

Self-studyWhy the order matters, not just the coverage4 min▶

Meeting everyone is necessary; meeting them in the right order is what makes it work. Sunniva starts with Kenji so her sponsor never hears anything second-hand. Then Bram, because Odile and Marguerite both ask him first: if his concerns are already in the plan, his "it is workable" reaches them before her slides do. Odile next, with Bram's changes visible. Rafe after that, because the trade with him is the largest and she wants Odile's yes in hand when she makes it. Halvard last before the room, because the cost line he reviews should already include every change the others asked for.

Each conversation changes the proposal, so later people see a better version. That is the quiet advantage of sequencing: the signer is not shown the first draft, they are shown the fifth, and the fifth has answers to questions they would have asked.

Self-studyWhat the bench showed, and what it did not3 min▶

Session 4 ran the ordering question on real ties from Krackhardt's 21 managers, not on Fernhollow. At the default settings (advice ties, a 65% threshold, three people pre-wired), starting at the top of the org chart brought 7 of 21 managers behind the proposal; starting with the brokers brought all 21 of 21. That is a modelled result on one small firm's advice network, and it shows the shape of the argument: who you start with decides how far the idea travels.

It does not tell you Fernhollow's numbers. Fernhollow is constructed and has no measured network. Use the bench as a reason to find your own brokers, never as a forecast of your own outcome.

Real world

The bench's ordering lesson, on real data. On Krackhardt's (1987) 21 managers, the model in session 4 found the org-chart start reached 7 of 21 and the broker start reached 21 of 21 at the default settings; when the main broker left, the old broker plan fell to 9 of 20 and a re-mapped plan reached 20 of 20. Those numbers belong to that firm's advice ties only. The lesson that travels is the method: sequence from the people others ask, and re-map when one of them leaves.

Then run the meeting well. Pre-wiring decides what people bring into the room. How you run the room itself, the agenda, the opening, getting to a decision, is taught in Facilitation ↗. The comms plan and the steering cadence that follow approval live in Data PMO, Stakeholders and steering ↗.

Part 2 · covers currencies of exchange

What each person trades in

5 min live

A one-to-one is not a briefing; it is a trade. Each person gives something the proposal needs (data, staff time, a word to the signer) and wants something back. The mistake data leaders make is paying everyone in the same coin, usually a better argument. Cohen and Bradford's model, reported here from summaries, says to find out what each person actually values and pay in that.

LiveSix steps, and the one that does the work3 min▶

Cohen and Bradford's Influence Without Authority (3rd edition, Wiley 2017) is reported here, not read: the model below comes from the publisher's press release. It has six steps: assume everyone is a potential ally; clarify your goals and priorities; diagnose the other person's world; identify relevant currencies; deal with relationships; influence through give and take.

Most of the effort sits in step 3. Session 2 already did it for Fernhollow: what each executive is measured on and what the platform costs them. Step 4 turns that diagnosis into a currency, the thing this person values enough to trade for. Rafe's fear is losing control of "his" CRM, so his currency is control. Offering him a faster report would be paying in the wrong coin.

Self-studyFive currency families, and Fernhollow's4 min▶

Summaries of the book group currencies into five families: inspiration (vision, doing the right thing), task (resources, help, a problem solved), position (recognition, visibility, control), relationship (being consulted, belonging) and personal (being thanked, ownership of an idea). These family names are reported from summaries; they were not read in the book itself, so treat the list as a prompt for thinking, not a fixed taxonomy.

Mapped onto Fernhollow (constructed): Rafe trades in position, specifically control. Bram trades in relationship and personal currency: nineteen years in, what he values is being consulted before a decision, not informed after it. Halvard trades in task: a cost line he can defend to the board and to audit. Odile trades in task too, but her task is depot time, so a capped cut-over is worth more to her than any benefit slide.

Self-studyWhat you offer must be yours to give2 min▶

Sunniva can offer Rafe CRM ownership only because Kenji agreed, in week 1, that the platform would read from existing systems rather than replace them. Every offer in the exchange table has to be checked with the person who owns it before it is made. An offer you cannot deliver is the fastest way to lose the trust session 2 built.

Trades, not negotiation tactics. Separating interests from positions, knowing your walk-away (BATNA) and trading across issues are taught properly in Negotiation, Positions and interests ↗ and Trade across issues ↗. Here the trade is small and repeated: you will work with these people for years, so every exchange has to leave them glad they made it.

Part 3 · covers persuasion research and the ethics line

Where influence ends and manipulation starts

5 min live

Persuasion research gives names to things people already do, and some of those names are better supported than others. More useful than any principle is one question, asked before every move: would this still work if they knew exactly what I was doing? Influence passes that test. Manipulation fails it, by design.

LiveSeven principles, and how sure we are about each3 min▶

Cialdini's Influence (new and expanded edition, 2021) names seven principles, as listed on influenceatwork.com: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity and unity. They are widely taught and widely quoted, and they are useful as a vocabulary for noticing what is going on in a conversation.

The evidence behind them is not uniform, and it would be wrong to resolve that here. The clearest caution is on commitment: Gamian-Wilk and Dolinski (2019) directly replicated the classic foot-in-the-door study, where agreeing to a small request makes people more likely to agree to a larger one, and found no significant effect in Poland or in Ukraine. Their abstract also says meta-analytic effects for the technique are small. That is one technique under one principle. It does not show the other six fail, and it does not show they hold. Treat each principle as a hypothesis about the person in front of you, not a lever that works on everyone.

Self-studyThe line, said out loud4 min▶

The professional norm Sunniva works to: influence states its real interest and survives being said out loud. Manipulation depends on the other person not knowing what you are doing. Three common examples fail the test every time: manufactured scarcity ("the budget window closes Friday" when it does not), fake social proof ("two depots have already signed up" when none have), and a favour given to create a debt the other person never agreed to.

The reason is practical as well as ethical. A data leader works with the same executives for years. Every manipulation is discovered eventually, usually by the person with the best memory in the building, and the cost lands on every later proposal. An influence move that is said out loud ("I need your account lists; in return the CRM stays yours") can be repeated next year with the same person, and it gets easier.

Self-studyWhen a principle is fine to use2 min▶

Reciprocity is fine when the favour is real and the ask is stated: Sunniva builds Bram a depot exception report he has wanted for years and tells him, plainly, that she will need two of his planners for the pilot. Social proof is fine when it is true: Odile hears that Bram has seen the plan because he has. Authority is fine when it is earned: Halvard's review carries weight in the room because he did review it. In each case, the move would still work if the other person knew everything.

Real world

A famous effect that did not replicate. Gamian-Wilk and Dolinski (2019), writing in Psychological Reports, repeated the foot-in-the-door procedure decades after the original studies and found no significant effect in either country they tested; their abstract notes that meta-analytic effects are small (abstract read). The practical lesson for a data leader is the one you would apply to any model: a well-known result is a starting assumption to check against the person in front of you, not a guarantee.

Build-along

Write the pre-wiring plan

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take the conversation order from your session 1 path. You will turn each name into one row of a plan: what they trade in, what you offer, what you ask, what you are willing to change, and the first sentence you will say. Sunniva's worked rows are in each box; write yours beside them. The Constructed Fernhollow Freight example is there to copy the shape, not the content.

Copy the order from your session 1 path. Your boss first, then the person asked first, outwards to the signer, the room last. If two people must hear it in the same week, decide who goes first and write why.

★ Step 1 · the orderWeek 1 0 Kenji sponsor: never hears it second-hand 1 Bram asked first by Odile and Marguerite Week 2 2 Odile with Bram's changes already in 3 Rafe biggest trade; Odile's yes in hand Week 3 4 Halvard cost line includes every change 5 the room ratifies; no first hearings

Name each person's currency. Start from session 2: what they are measured on and what the proposal costs them. The cost usually points straight at the currency.

★ Step 2 · currencyKenji position: no surprises, his sponsorship visible Bram relationship, personal: consulted, not informed Odile task: depot time protected Rafe position: control of "his" CRM Halvard task: a cost line he can defend

Write the offer, in their currency. Check every offer with whoever owns it before you make it. An offer you cannot keep costs more than no offer.

★ Step 3 · the offerKenji first look; his name on the paper Bram the draft before anyone; his depot rules in it Odile cut-over hours capped and costed Rafe platform reads from his CRM; ownership stays his (checked with Kenji in week 1) Halvard cost by year, a stop point after each phase

Write the ask, plainly. One concrete thing per person, something they can say yes to in the meeting. A vague ask ("your support") gets a vague answer.

★ Step 4 · the askKenji sponsor it at the executive meeting Bram tell Odile it is workable; two planners for the pilot Odile release depot staff for the pilot weeks Rafe the account lists, on a fixed date Halvard review the case before the meeting, not in it

Write the change you are willing to make. If nobody can change the proposal, you are briefing, not pre-wiring. Decide in advance what is negotiable and what is not.

★ Step 5 · what might changeNegotiable which depot goes first, cut-over timing, read from CRM instead of replacing it, phase gates and stop points Not negotiable one shared customer record across sales, depots and billing; first depot live by Q3

Write the sentence you open each one-to-one with. It should state your real interest in the first breath, so the conversation passes the "would it still work if they knew" test from the start.

★ Step 6 · the opening sentenceKenji "Before anyone else sees this, I want your view and your name on it." Bram "You know the depots better than anyone. What would make this workable?" Odile "Bram has seen this. Here is how we keep depot hours capped." Rafe "I need the account lists. In return, the CRM stays yours. What else would you need?" Halvard "Here is the cost by year, with a point where you can stop it."

Leave a blank row. Add one empty row at the bottom of your plan for the person you have not thought of. In most real plans, someone asks "has X seen this?" in week 2, and X goes into the empty row before the room meets.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Finish your plan. All six steps for a real proposal, with one row per one-to-one and a blank row at the end.
  2. Hold one one-to-one. Take the first row of your plan and have that conversation this week. Write down, the same day, what changed in the proposal because of it.
  3. Run the test on your own habits. List three persuasion moves you have used in the last month. For each, ask whether it would still have worked if the other person had known exactly what you were doing.
  4. Mark your fragile row. Which person in your plan, if they left or changed their mind, would break the sequence? Session 6 is about that moment.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does Sunniva hold the one-to-ones in a set order before the executive meeting?

Pre-wiring means nobody hears the proposal for the first time in the room, and the order lets every change carry forward. C contradicts the bench lesson from session 4; D would fail the influence test, since each person knowing the others have been asked is part of what makes it work.

2 · Rafe fears losing control of "his" CRM. In Cohen and Bradford's terms (reported), what does Sunniva do?

Currencies are what a person values enough to trade for, and Rafe's is control. A better argument is the wrong coin; going over his head spends trust; leaving him out ignores session 1, where he is the person who can stall the platform after it is approved.

3 · Which statement about persuasion is best supported by what you saw tonight?

Gamian-Wilk and Dolinski (2019) found no significant effect in Poland or Ukraine, which makes C the shakiest claim, but it says nothing about the other six principles, so A over-reaches. B fails the disclosure test whatever the goal. D states both the evidence and the line.
