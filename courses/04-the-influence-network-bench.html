The influence-network bench

Sessions 1 to 3 drew the influence route by asking people. Tonight we look at a real one. In 1987 David Krackhardt asked 21 managers at a small high-tech manufacturer who they went to for advice, who their friends were and who they reported to. Those ties are on the bench below, and every count and centrality is computed from them in your browser. On top of the real ties sits a stated model of how support spreads, so you can test the question every data leader faces before a big ask: if you can only have three conversations first, with whom?

Part 0

Where we start

Krackhardt's 1987 paper in Social Networks introduced cognitive social structures: instead of asking each person only about their own ties, he asked every manager to report the whole network as they saw it. The abstract describes the example as the management team of a small manufacturing firm; the UCINET dataset page reports a high-tech equipment maker on the US west coast with just over 100 employees. The bench uses the version most often analysed: each manager's own report of who they go to for advice, who they count as friends, and who they report to. 21 people, 190 advice ties, 102 friendship ties, 20 reporting lines. Manager 7 is the CEO; managers 2, 14, 18 and 21 are vice presidents; everyone else is a manager reporting to one of them.

Part 1 · covers in-degree, betweenness and the threshold rule

How ties become numbers

6 min live

Three numbers carry the bench. Two are measured from the ties and one is a model. Knowing which is which is the whole difference between reading the bench and being fooled by it.

LiveAsked by, and brokering, are not the same3 min▶

In-degree counts how many colleagues name a person as someone they go to for advice. It is a popularity count among people seeking help, and it is measured directly: no assumption beyond the survey itself.

Betweenness counts, for every pair of colleagues, the share of the shortest advice routes between them that pass through a person, and adds it up. A person with modest in-degree can have high betweenness if they connect groups that otherwise do not talk. That is the broker of session 1 and of Burt's structural holes work (abstract read): the person on the only short route between two parts of the organisation. The bench computes it with the standard algorithm on the directed advice ties, no weights, nothing normalised.

LiveThe threshold rule, and why it is labelled a model3 min▶

Nobody in Krackhardt's firm was ever asked about Sunniva's platform. To ask "how far would support spread?" the bench needs a rule, and it uses the linear threshold idea from Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos (2003, read at source): a person comes on board once enough of the people who influence them already have. Here, a manager backs the proposal once at least a set share of the people they go to for advice back it. You pre-wire a few people; the rule runs in rounds until nobody else moves.

Kempe and colleagues give every person their own threshold, drawn at random. The bench gives everyone one shared threshold, set with a slider, so the results are exact and repeatable. That is a simplification real people do not obey. Read the ORDER of the strategies on the bench, which survives most settings, and never the exact count as a forecast for your own company.

Read it in this order. The tag first (measured or modelled), then the verdict line, then the comparison table. The strongest lesson is not the 21: it is that swapping vice president 14, who runs the biggest team, for vice president 18, who sits on the most routes, changes the result from 7 backers to all of them.

Part 2 · covers what the 21 managers' ties show

Asked, senior and brokering are three different people

6 min live

Put the formal chart next to the two measured numbers and three different people come out on top. The most-asked manager is a vice president, 2. The top broker is a different vice president, 18. The CEO, 7, is asked by 13 colleagues, the same as manager 1, who has no one reporting to him. And the vice president with the biggest team, 14, brokers almost nothing.

LiveWhat a data leader does with this3 min▶

At Fernhollow, Sunniva cannot survey 21 managers before her proposal, and should not try (session 6 explains why network surveys need consent and care). What she can do is the session 1 question, asked in one-to-ones: "Who do you check this kind of thing with?" Names that come back from people in different departments are candidate brokers. Names that come back many times from the same department are the locally most-asked. The bench says those are different people, and that the brokers carry a proposal further.

The seniority lesson is narrower than it sounds. The CEO is the third broker here, and the brokers' plan includes him. What fails is starting from the chart: the CEO plus the vice president with the biggest team reached 7 of 21 because that vice president sits on almost no routes between other people.

Self-studyOne person who asks only one person2 min▶

Manager 6 names exactly one person as a source of advice: vice president 21. On the bench that makes manager 6 easy to move (one backer is 100 percent of their advisers) and completely dependent on one relationship. Every organisation has people like this, often the longest-serving, and they are the first to feel abandoned when their one link leaves. The break button removes 18, not 21, so on the bench nobody is cut off; in your own map, look for the person with one line.

Real world

Weak ties travel further. Granovetter's 1973 paper argued that the people you know less well, the weak ties, are more likely to connect you to parts of the network your close circle does not reach (abstract read). A broker is the organisational version: the person whose acquaintances span departments. At Krackhardt's firm, vice president 18 runs a team of two and sits on more shortest advice routes than anyone else.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever, the break button and the model's limits

What breaks the plan, and what the bench cannot tell you

6 min live

A bench that can only ever report good news measures nothing. This one has two ways to report a worse number: the anti-lever, which you have already run, and the break button, which takes the most important person out of the network and re-runs your plan without them.

LiveThe anti-lever, honestly sized2 min▶

At the default, pre-wiring the top of the chart reaches 7 of 21 and the brokers reach 21. That gap is real for these ties and this rule, and it is not universal. Set the threshold to 30 percent and every strategy reaches 21: when everyone moves easily, it does not matter who you start with. Set pre-wire to 1 and the threshold to 50 or lower and the CEO alone (2 backers) beats vice president 18 alone (1). The defensible claim is the one the comparison table shows across the middle of the slider: starting from the chart is never better than starting from the brokers once you have two or three conversations to spend.

LiveAdvice ties and friendship ties disagree3 min▶

Switch the bench to friendship ties and the map changes. The top friendship broker is manager 17, not 18; the most-named friends are 2, 1 and 12. At the default, the most-named pick reaches 8 and the brokers 3, and the rule fails for every chart, popularity and broker plan, because the CEO named no one as a friend: on friendship ties he can only back the proposal if you pre-wire him personally.

Krackhardt's own argument, in later work on the same tradition, was that the friendship network matters for anticipating political resistance (reported). The bench does not settle which network carries a decision; that depends on the decision. A budget call travels on advice; a reorganisation that threatens people's colleagues may travel on friendship. Ask which ties your decision rides on before you trust either map.

Self-studyWhat the model leaves out4 min▶

Threshold models are a simplification, and the disagreement is worth knowing. They treat influence as counting heads among your contacts; real people weigh a trusted adviser above three acquaintances, change their minds back, and respond to the content of a proposal, not just its supporters. Kempe and colleagues' version draws a different random threshold for each person; this bench uses one shared threshold so the numbers are exact, which makes cascades more all-or-nothing than real organisations are.

The ties are also old and self-reported: one firm, one moment, 21 people, as each person described their own ties. The 1987 paper itself was about how differently people perceive the same network, which is a caution against trusting any one person's map, yours included. What survives these limits is the ordering lesson, and it matches the qualitative research on brokers: where you start matters, and the formal chart is a poor guide to where to start.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Find your three brokers. Go back to your session 1 path. Which names came up from people in different departments? Those are your candidate brokers. Which names came up many times from one department? Those are the locally most-asked.
  2. Run the bench at your threshold. Is your organisation quick to move (try 40 percent) or slow (try 75)? Note how the comparison table changes, and which strategy holds up.
  3. Plan for one exit. If your top broker left next month, who would you re-map to? Name them now.
  4. Pick your own three on the bench before looking at the strategy buttons, then compare. Write one sentence on why your pick did better or worse.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On advice ties at the default, why does the brokers' plan (18, 21, 7) reach all 21 while the org chart's plan (7, 14, 21) reaches 7?

Both are vice presidents, so seniority is equal; the most-asked person is 2, not 18; and the CEO is in both plans. The only difference is the swap of 14 for 18, and betweenness is what separates them.

2 · Which statement about the bench's numbers is accurate?

Nobody in the 1987 firm was asked about any proposal. The ties are real; the spread is a model with one shared threshold, which real people do not obey. The slider changes backers, never betweenness.

3 · Manager 18 leaves. The brokers' plan falls from 21 of 21 to 9 of 20. What is the right reading?

No pair is cut off (0), so B is wrong; 43 ordered pairs just need longer routes. The chart's plan still reaches only 9. A changed network needs a new map, and the re-mapped brokers carry it again.
