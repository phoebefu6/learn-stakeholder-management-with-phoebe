Losing a stakeholder, and the long game

Approval is not the end of stakeholder work; it is the moment the stakes go up. Four months after her platform is approved, Sunniva's team ships a wrong number to the executive pack, the COO quotes it to the board, and two relationships start to cool. Tonight we separate the two ways trust breaks, read what one well-known study found about apology and denial (and what it does not license), and write the one-page plan that keeps a stakeholder map alive for years rather than one approval.

Part 0

Where we start

Constructed Fernhollow Freight example. The platform was approved. Four months later, Sunniva's team publishes the depot on-time rate in the monthly executive pack, and the figure is wrong: a join double-counted re-deliveries. Odile, the COO, quoted it to the board before anyone noticed. Since then Odile has been taking depot questions to her own analysts and leaving Sunniva out. And Rafe, who agreed in session 5 to send the account lists on a fixed date, has quietly stopped sending them. Nobody has said a word in a meeting. Two stakeholders are leaving the relationship without leaving the building.

Part 1 · covers the two kinds of trust violation

How you lose someone

5 min live

Trust breaks in two different ways. A competence violation says "you are not as good at this as I thought": a wrong number, a missed date, a model that drifted. An integrity violation says "you are not as honest as I thought": a hidden problem, a broken promise, being seen to go around someone. The two feel similar on the day and heal very differently afterwards, and the most expensive mistake a data leader makes is turning the first into the second.

LiveTwo ways to lose someone3 min▶

Competence. Odile now doubts that Sunniva's numbers are right. That is serious for a head of data, but it is a doubt about skill and process, and skill and process can be shown to improve: a check that would have caught the double count, a second pair of eyes on any figure that goes to the board, a month of packs that come out clean.

Integrity. If Odile comes to believe Sunniva knew and said nothing, the doubt moves from "can she?" to "will she tell me?". No new check fixes that, because the check would be run by the person she no longer believes. Being seen to go around someone belongs here too: if Sunniva had quietly sent the corrected figure to Marguerite before telling Odile, she would have added an integrity problem to a competence one, with the person who quoted the number.

Self-studyHow a competence loss becomes an integrity loss4 min▶

Most losses for data leaders start as competence: a pipeline breaks, a definition changes, a join goes wrong. They become integrity losses through the handling, and three handlings do it almost every time. Hiding it, hoping the next refresh quietly corrects it. Letting it surface elsewhere, so the stakeholder hears it from finance, from the board or from their own analyst. And softening it into something it was not, "a minor reconciliation difference", when it changed a number someone repeated in public.

The test for a data leader is short: would this person rather hear it from me, today, than from anyone else, later? The answer is almost always yes, and the cost of telling them first is almost always smaller than the cost of them finding out.

Rafe's silence is a different case and worth separating. Nothing at Fernhollow says Sunniva broke a promise to him. Before she decides what kind of loss it is, she needs to find out what changed for him: a new target, a new boss, or the same wrong number reaching him second-hand through Odile.

The message is taught elsewhere. How to write and deliver the bad-news message itself, the structure, the timing, the first sentence, is taught in Communication, Bad news and disagreement ↗. This session is about the relationship after the message has been sent: what kind of damage it was, and how the trust is rebuilt over the following months.

Part 2 · covers Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks (2004)

Apology or denial: what the evidence says

5 min live

A well-known experiment on repairing trust after a violation is Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks, "Removing the shadow of suspicion" (Journal of Applied Psychology, 2004). Its abstract, which is what was read for these pages, reports that the right response depends on the type of violation. It is a useful finding and a narrow one, and both halves matter.

LiveWhat the study found3 min▶

From the abstract: trust was repaired more when the person apologised for a competence-based violation, and more when they denied culpability for an integrity-based one. The study also looked at what happened when later evidence came in. An apology repaired more when that evidence showed the person was guilty; a denial repaired more when it showed they were innocent.

The method, as reported in the Washington University press release rather than read in the paper: business students played the role of people hiring, and judged a job candidate who had been accused of an error on a tax return. The participant counts were not read for these pages, so none are given here.

The reasoning usually given is that an apology admits the act. For a lapse of skill, admitting it costs little, because people expect skill to be improvable. For a lapse of honesty, admitting it confirms the very thing that is hardest to win back.

Self-studyWhat it does not license4 min▶

Read the limits before using the finding. It is a laboratory study. The people judging were students, not executives who have worked with the person for years. The hiring decision was hypothetical, the violation was an accusation about one candidate, and the relationship had no history and no future. A real COO who has just repeated a wrong number to her own board is in none of those conditions.

The study compares responses to an accusation. It does not show that denying something you actually did is a good repair strategy, and the later-evidence result points the other way: when the evidence later showed guilt, the apology did better. For a data leader the evidence usually arrives quickly, because the corrected figure is the evidence.

Where researchers and practitioners still disagree is how far lab findings on strangers carry over to long working relationships, and how often real violations are cleanly one type. These pages do not settle that. They use the finding as a prompt to name the violation type before choosing a response.

Self-studyHow Sunniva applies it3 min▶

The wrong on-time rate is a competence violation: her team made an error of skill and process, and nobody hid it. So the honest repair, and the one the study's competence row points to, is an apology with a fix. Not "the source system was inconsistent", which is a partial denial dressed as an explanation; not silence until the next pack corrects it.

The apology names what was wrong and accepts it as her team's. The fix says what has already been corrected. The change says what is different from now on, so the same error cannot reach the board twice. Written, so Odile can forward it upwards if she needs to. The build-along writes it.

Real world

"70 percent of change programmes fail." Executives quote this in steering meetings to explain why a programme is struggling. Hughes (2011), in the Journal of Change Management, reviewed five published instances of the claim and found no valid, reliable empirical evidence behind any of them (abstract read). A stakeholder who quotes it is quoting a number with no source. A data leader is the person in the room who should check before the number travels, the same discipline that would have caught Fernhollow's double count.

Part 3 · covers maintaining a stakeholder system

The long game

5 min live

A stakeholder map is a snapshot. People leave, reorganisations move budgets, a quiet manager becomes the person everyone asks. The map that won the approval starts to decay the day it is approved. The long game is a small routine, repeated, that keeps the map true and the trust topped up, so the next proposal starts from a relationship instead of from cold.

LiveRe-map after any exit or reorganisation3 min▶

Session 4's break button made the point on real data. On Krackhardt's advice ties for 21 managers, the brokers' plan reached 21 of 21. When manager 18, the top broker, left, the same plan reached 9 of 20 and the decision rule failed. Re-mapped on the 20 who remained, it reached 20 of 20. Those are modelled backers on real ties, not a forecast for any company, and they are Krackhardt's managers, never Fernhollow's.

The lesson carries over without the model: the map is only as current as its last re-draw. At Fernhollow, if Bram retired, the first question would be who Odile and Marguerite now ask about the depots, and Sunniva would want to know before they did. The session 1 question still does the work: "Who do you check this kind of thing with now?"

Self-studyA cadence of small kept promises3 min▶

Session 2 used Maister, Green and Galford's trust equation (a practitioner model) as a checklist, and reliability was the part a new hire has least of. Reliability is built in small, kept, visible promises far more than in one big delivery: "the corrected pack by Thursday", "the depot pilot dates before your planning meeting", "a one-line note if anything in your numbers moves".

One promise per person per quarter is enough if each is dated, small enough to be certain, and closed out loud. The repair after a wrong number is built the same way: Odile does not need a grand gesture, she needs three clean packs in a row and a note each time saying the new check ran.

Self-studyKeep paying in each person's currency3 min▶

Session 5 found each person's currency, using Cohen and Bradford's exchange framing (reported). Currencies do not expire at approval, and a relationship that is only paid when you need something reads as transactional by the second year. Rafe trades in control of "his" CRM; Odile in depot time; Halvard in a credible cost; Bram in being consulted before a decision rather than informed after it.

Currencies also move. If Rafe's target changed this year, control may matter less to him than a retention list his account managers can use. That is why the loop checks each scorecard before choosing what to offer.

Self-studyA note on mapping people2 min▶

Asking colleagues "who do you go to for advice?" in a one-to-one is ordinary management. Running it as a survey across a department, or inferring it from email and calendar data, creates sensitive information about named people: who is consulted, who is ignored, who is isolated. Organisational network surveys need consent, a clear statement of what the data is for and who sees it, and care about how results are shown. Keep your own stakeholder map private, written in terms you would be comfortable having the person read.

Cadence, not a comms plan. The steering cadence and the communications plan for a running programme are taught in Data PMO, Stakeholders and steering ↗ and Stakeholder management ↗. The loop here sits underneath them: it keeps the people behind the plan known and the trust current, whatever the programme's meeting rhythm is.

Real world

One person leaves, half the plan goes. In Krackhardt's (1987) study of 21 managers at a small high-tech manufacturer, the person with the highest betweenness on advice ties was a vice president, manager 18. On the session 4 bench, removing that one manager took the brokers' plan from 21 of 21 backers to 9 of 20, and re-mapping recovered 20 of 20. Every number is computed on the study's own ties under a stated, simplified model; the size of the drop is the model's, the direction is the lesson.

Build-along

Write the stakeholder operating plan

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The capstone. One page that pulls together the six sessions for the people around your work: who decides, who brokers, what each person is measured on and trades in, where trust stands, what needs repair, and when you will look again. Sunniva's version is on the left of every step; write yours beside it. If your map is still thin, a thin honest page beats a full guessed one.

Write the four roles from session 1. Refresh them: approval is behind you, so ask who must keep saying yes (budget renewals, phase gates) and who can still stall it by not moving.

★ Step 1 · the mapMust say yes Marguerite (phase gates), Halvard (year-2 spend), Kenji (runs the platform) Can say no Odile (depot time), Rafe (the account lists) Asked first Bram (Odile and Marguerite both ask him) Lives with it depot planners, account managers, billing

Name the brokers, and the backup if one leaves. For each person who carries a view to a decider, write who you would re-map to if they went. Session 4 showed what happens to a plan that skips this.

★ Step 2 · brokers and backupBram carries depot views to Odile and Marguerite if he leaves: ask Odile who she asks now; ask the two pilot planners who they go to Halvard carries cost views to Marguerite if he leaves: meet the new CFO before the next gate

Write each person's scorecard and currency. From sessions 2 and 5, checked again now. Mark anything that has moved since approval.

★ Step 3 · scorecard and currencyOdile on-time delivery, depot productivity | depot time Rafe revenue, retention, key accounts | control (CRM) Halvard cost to serve, capex discipline | a credible cost Kenji uptime, delivery of the IT roadmap | no surprises Bram the depots run | being consulted Moved? Odile: the board now quotes our numbers in her name

Write the repair entry for anyone you have lost. Name the violation type first. For a competence violation, the entry is an apology with the fix, written, sent before anyone else tells them. Four parts: what was wrong, the cause, the fix, what changes so it cannot recur.

★ Step 4 · repair: Sunniva to OdileType: competence (wrong number), not yet integrity. Keep it that way. Odile, The depot on-time rate in last month's executive pack was wrong, and I am sorry: it was my team's error, and you repeated it to the board on our word. Cause: a join counted re-deliveries twice. Fix: the corrected figure and the board-ready line are attached. What changes: every figure that goes to the board now runs a second-person check against the depot source, and you see the depot numbers 48 hours before the pack goes out. I would value 20 minutes this week on anything else you want checked. Sunniva

Plan a re-engagement for anyone who has gone quiet. Find out what changed before deciding what it is. Then offer something in their currency, not a reminder of what they owe you.

★ Step 5 · Rafe: re-engagementFirst, find out: new target? new boss? did the wrong number reach him through Odile? Then, in his currency (control): "Rafe, the account lists stopped coming last month. Before I chase anything: would it help if your team set the sync rules for their accounts, so nothing leaves the CRM you have not seen?" Not: a note to Kenji, or an escalation to Marguerite.

Set the review date and one promise per person for next quarter. Small, dated, certain. A promise you might miss does more damage than no promise at all.

★ Step 6 · review and promisesReview first week of next quarter; also within 2 weeks of any exit or reorganisation Odile three clean packs, each with "check ran" noted Rafe sync rules agreed with his team by month 2 Halvard year-2 cost line with the stop point, before the gate Kenji a monthly one-line status, no surprises Bram pilot planners' feedback shown to him before Odile

Keep it to one page. A plan that needs scrolling will not be reread every quarter, and a plan that is not reread goes stale the same way a map does. If it runs over, the scorecard section is usually carrying detail that belongs in your notes, not on the page.

Homework

After the last session

30 min
  1. Finish your operating plan. All six sections for your own executives. Put the review date in your calendar before you close the page.
  2. Sort one past loss. Think of a stakeholder who cooled on you. Was it competence or integrity, and did the handling move it from one to the other? Write one line on what you would do differently.
  3. Find your single point of failure. Name the one broker whose exit would hurt your plan most, and write today who you would re-map to.
  4. Make and keep one small promise. Pick the stakeholder with the thinnest reliability, make one dated promise this week, and close it out loud.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks (2004) find, and what is the honest way to use it?

The abstract reports that the best response depended on the violation type, and that later evidence of guilt favoured apology. A and B ignore the type; D invents a method. The study used business students judging a hypothetical hire (reported), so it is a prompt to name the violation type, not a rule for real relationships.

2 · Sunniva's team ships a wrong number. Why would staying quiet about it make things worse than the error itself?

The error is a competence violation: skill and process, which can be shown to improve. Concealment adds an integrity violation, a doubt about honesty, which a check run by the same person cannot answer. A misreads the type; B and C are invented.

3 · A broker your plan depends on leaves the company. What does session 4's bench suggest you do?

The plan as it stood failed once the broker was gone; re-mapping on the people who remained recovered it. The numbers are modelled on real ties, not a forecast, but the direction is clear: a map goes stale the day a broker leaves, so redraw it.
