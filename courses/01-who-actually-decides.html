Learn Stakeholder Management with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Who actually decides

Every proposal that needs other people's money, data or time has two maps. The org chart says who signs. The decision itself travels a different route: through the person the signer always asks first, past the person who can quietly stall it, and only then to the signature. Tonight we separate authority from influence, draw the real route over the chart for one constructed company, and sort everyone around a decision into four roles you can name before you book a single meeting.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Fernhollow Freight is an invented mid-size logistics company. Sunniva Holm joined three months ago as head of data, reporting to the CIO, and wants one customer-data platform approved: one shared customer record across sales, the depots and billing, instead of the three that disagree today. Five executives and one long-serving depot planning manager stand between her and a yes. Everything Sunniva does across the six sessions is a move you can make with your own executives.

Part 1 · covers authority against influence

The chart says who signs

5 min live

Authority is the formal right to say yes or no: a budget line, a signature, a seat on the committee that approves. Influence is the ability to change what the people with authority believe before they use it. A proposal needs both, but they live in different places. Authority is printed on the org chart. Influence is not printed anywhere, which is why a data leader who only reads the chart keeps being surprised.

LiveAuthority and influence are different things3 min▶

Authority is a right. Kenji, the CIO, can put the platform in next year's IT budget or leave it out. Halvard, the CFO, can send the business case back. Marguerite, the CEO, signs anything above the capital threshold. Those three can each stop the proposal, and the chart tells you so.

Influence is a capability. Bram has run depot planning for nineteen years and has no budget and no signature. But when Marguerite wants to know whether the depots can absorb a change, she asks Bram, and when Odile, the COO, has to say yes to anything that touches depot systems, she asks Bram too. If Bram thinks the platform means a winter of double data entry, two executives hear that before they hear Sunniva. Nothing on the chart warns her.

Self-studyWhy a data leader is especially exposed3 min▶

Most data and AI proposals ask other teams to change how they work: enter data differently, give up a spreadsheet they control, trust a number they did not produce. The cost lands on people who did not ask for the change and who usually sit outside the requester's reporting line. That makes the influence route longer than for, say, a new laptop policy, and it puts the people who live with the change (depot planners, account managers) close to the people who advise the signers.

A data lead also tends to arrive with the strongest argument in the room and the weakest network, especially in the first year. Tonight's work is to make the network visible before the argument is made.

Real world

The company behind the chart. Krackhardt and Hanson's 1993 Harvard Business Review article argued that informal networks, who people go to for advice and who they trust, bypass the reporting lines on the chart, and that mapping them helps managers see how work really gets done (summary read on the HBR page). Session 4 opens the data from Krackhardt's own 1987 study of 21 managers at a small high-tech manufacturer: the most-asked manager was a vice president, not the CEO, and an ordinary manager was asked as often as the CEO, 13 colleagues each.

Part 2 · covers the real decision path

The route a decision really travels

5 min live

The formal route for Sunniva's proposal is short: she writes it up, Kenji sponsors it, the executive committee discusses it, Marguerite signs. The real route has more stops and a different order. Drawing it over the chart is the single most useful hour a data leader spends before asking for anything.

LiveReading the path, stop by stop3 min▶

1 · Bram. Asked first by both Odile and Marguerite on anything touching the depots. If he has seen the plan and his concerns are in it, the first thing two executives hear is "it is workable". 2 · Odile. She can say no on behalf of depot time, and she decides after Bram. 3 · Halvard. He must say yes to the spend, and Marguerite asks him before she signs, so he is a gate and a voice. 4 · Rafe. He has no formal veto, but the platform needs his account lists, and a director who withholds data can stall a programme for a year without ever saying no. 5 · Marguerite. She signs after hearing Halvard and Odile.

Kenji, Sunniva's boss and the formal sponsor, is not a stop on the influence route at all. He is the person who must never be surprised by any of it, which is session 3.

Self-studyHow to find the path when nobody tells you4 min▶

Three questions do most of the work. For each person who must say yes: "When you get a proposal like this, who do you check it with?" For each person whose work changes: "Who would you want to have seen this before it goes up?" And for the last similar proposal that succeeded or died: "What happened between the paper and the decision?" Ask them in one-to-ones, not in a meeting, and write the answers down the same day.

Watch for the names that come up in more than one answer. A person who sits on several routes at once is what session 4 calls a broker, and the bench shows, on 21 real managers, how much more a conversation with a broker carries than a conversation with the most senior person in the room.

Map, then plan. The power/interest grid and the comms plan built on it are taught in Data PMO, Stakeholders and steering ↗ and Stakeholder management ↗. They tell you how much attention each person needs. The path tells you the order the attention has to arrive in. Use both; neither replaces the other.

Part 3 · covers the four roles around a decision

Must say yes, can say no, asked first, lives with it

5 min live

Every person around a decision fits at least one of four roles. Naming them before you ask for anything stops the two classic mistakes: lobbying the signer while the person who can stall it hears about it last, and ignoring the people who will live with the result until they resist it.

LiveWhy "can say no" is bigger than it looks3 min▶

Formal vetoes are rare. Practical vetoes are everywhere: the director whose data you need, the operations lead whose people must change a process, the security team who can take six months to review. None of them has to say no in a meeting. They just do not move, and an approved programme stalls.

The test is simple: could this person, by doing nothing, make the project fail after it is approved? If yes, they belong in the second column, whatever their title. At Fernhollow, Rafe has no seat on the approval, and he is the single most likely reason the platform is approved and then never fed.

Self-studyThis is not RACI, and not decision rights inside a team3 min▶

RACI assigns roles to tasks once a programme exists: who is responsible, accountable, consulted, informed. It is taught in Data PMO, RACI and R&R ↗. The four roles here come earlier: they describe the route to getting the programme approved at all, and they include people RACI would never list, like the planning manager everyone asks.

Decision rights inside your own team, who on your team decides what, are taught in Team Management, Who decides what ↗. These pages are about decisions that cross teams and go up.

Real world

Brokers get the credit, and the hearing. In a study of managers at a large American electronics company, Burt (2004) reported that people whose networks spanned groups that did not otherwise talk to each other were more likely to have their ideas judged valuable, and less likely to have them dismissed (abstract read). The "asked first" column is often where those people sit. Session 4 measures it on real ties.

Build-along

Map one decision's path

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take one proposal you need approved in the next six months. If you have none, use Sunniva's platform. You will work backwards from the signature to the first conversation, then turn the path around into the order you will actually talk to people. Paper is fine; the shape matters more than the tool.

Write the decision as one line with a yes or no answer. "Improve customer data" is a topic. A decision is something a person can sign.

★ Step 1 · the decisionApprove a two-year customer-data platform: one shared customer record across sales, depots and billing, budget in next year's IT plan, first depot live by Q3. Test: could Marguerite sign or refuse this on one page? yes

List who must say yes. Read it off the chart and the approval rules: budget holders, the signer, any committee. Write what each one is saying yes to.

★ Step 2 · must say yesMarguerite (CEO) signs above the capital threshold Halvard (CFO) the business case and the spend Kenji (CIO) a line in the IT budget, and running it

List who can say no, formally or by not moving. Ask of each name: could they make it fail after approval by doing nothing?

★ Step 3 · can say noRafe (Commercial) the account lists live in "his" CRM Odile (COO) depot staff time during the cut-over (not Kenji: he is a yes, and Sunniva's boss)

For every name so far, write who they ask first. If you do not know, that is the finding, and the first one-to-one you book is to find out.

★ Step 4 · asked firstMarguerite asks Halvard (numbers), Odile (operations) Odile asks Bram (depot planning, 19 years) Halvard asks his finance business partner (unknown: find out) Rafe asks his top two account managers (unknown)

Add who lives with it. The people whose daily work changes. They rarely decide anything, and they are who the "asked first" people listen to.

★ Step 5 · lives with itDepot planners enter delivery exceptions in a new screen Account managers stop keeping their own customer spreadsheets Billing team one customer id instead of three

Turn the path into an order. Your boss first, so nothing reaches them second-hand. Then from the person asked first, outwards, to the signer last. Nobody should hear the proposal for the first time in the meeting that decides it.

★ Step 6 · conversation order0 Kenji before anyone: no surprises for the sponsor 1 Bram what would make this workable in a depot? 2 Odile with Bram's concerns already in the plan 3 Rafe what he keeps control of; what he gets 4 Halvard the numbers, with Odile's yes noted 5 the room a decision, not a first hearing

Circle the repeat names. Anyone who appears in two or more lists of step 4 is on several routes at once. That person is worth more of your time than their title suggests, and session 4 shows why, on real data.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Finish your path. All six steps for a real proposal. If step 4 is mostly "unknown", that is your list of one-to-ones for next week.
  2. Trace one past decision. Pick a cross-team proposal from the last year that was approved or died. Who was asked before it reached the signer? Compare that with what the chart would have predicted.
  3. Find one practical veto. Name one person who could stall your proposal after approval without ever saying no. What do they need to see first?
  4. Bring your "must say yes" list. Session 2 adds, for each name, what they are measured on and what your proposal costs them.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Bram has no budget and no signature at Fernhollow. Why does Sunniva talk to him first?

Bram has influence, not authority: Odile and Marguerite both check depot changes with him. A is about use, not route; B confuses courtesy with the path; D invents a veto he does not have. His view arrives first, so it should already contain hers.

2 · Which person belongs in the "can say no" column?

Formal vetoes are rare; practical ones are common. Rafe has no seat on the approval, but a director who withholds the account data can stall the platform for a year. Seniority and attendance are not the test; the ability to stall is.

3 · How is the decision path different from a power/interest grid or a RACI?

The grid (Data PMO) sizes attention, RACI assigns work after approval, and the path gives the order of conversations before it. Each answers a different question, so a data leader uses all three.
