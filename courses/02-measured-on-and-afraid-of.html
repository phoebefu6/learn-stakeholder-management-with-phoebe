Learn Stakeholder Management with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

What they are measured on, and what they fear

Session 1 told you who has to say yes and in what order. Tonight asks what each of those people is weighing when you walk in. Every executive judges your proposal against their own scorecard, not yours. A yes always costs them something, even when the budget is somebody else's, and the cost is often a fear they will never say out loud. And under all of it sits a quieter question: how much do they trust you yet?

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Fernhollow Freight is an invented mid-size logistics company, and Sunniva Holm is its head of data, three months in, reporting to Kenji Arai, the CIO. Her proposal is one customer-data platform: one shared customer record across sales, depots and billing, two years, budget in next year's IT plan, first depot live by Q3. Last session she mapped the route. Tonight she finds out that every stop on that route is doing its own private arithmetic, and that hers is not the arithmetic that counts.

Part 1 · covers what each executive is measured on

Every executive has a scorecard

5 min live

Nobody in the room is measured on "better data". The CFO is measured on cost and capital discipline, the COO on deliveries going out on time, the commercial director on revenue. When a data leader presents a platform, each of them silently translates it into their own two or three numbers and asks one question: does this move my numbers, or put them at risk? If you have not done that translation for them, they do it themselves, usually less kindly.

LiveReading a scorecard from public signals3 min▶

Most executives never hand you their objectives, but they leave them lying around. Four places are almost always open to a head of data. The board pack, or whatever summary goes up to the board: the numbers an executive reports upward are the numbers they are judged on. All-hands and town halls: what each executive chooses to talk about when they get ten minutes. Their own team's KPIs: dashboards, weekly reports and targets their direct reports chase. And what they bring up first in any meeting, before the agenda starts: that is the number keeping them awake.

Sunniva did this in her first month without calling it stakeholder work. Halvard opens every finance review with cost to serve per delivery. Odile's depot boards show on-time percentage in the largest font on the wall. Rafe talks about the top twenty accounts by name. Two metrics per person was enough to change how she wrote the business case.

Self-studyWhy your proposal is judged on someone else's metrics4 min▶

A data platform is infrastructure: its benefit shows up in other people's numbers, a little later, and shared across several of them. That makes it unusually easy to argue for in general and unusually hard for any one executive to own. Each of them sees a slice of the benefit and the whole of their own risk.

The practical consequence is a rule, not a slogan: write one sentence per executive that names their metric, and only then name the platform. "Cost to serve per customer, measured on one record instead of three" lands with Halvard. "A single customer record" lands with nobody, because nobody is measured on it.

The marketing, brand and business-development version of this, reading a partner team's scorecard before you ask anything of it, is taught in Marketing Analytics, the partner's scorecard ↗. Use it when your stakeholders sit in marketing; the move here is the same one, applied upward to five executives at once.

Real world

The numbers a leader reports are the numbers that judge them. Public companies publish this in the open: annual reports name the measures that set executive bonuses, and investor presentations open with the three or four numbers management wants to be judged on. A data leader inside any company can do the same reading on the internal equivalents. The point is not the specific measures, which differ everywhere; it is that they are written down somewhere you can read before you ask for anything.

Part 2 · covers the cost of saying yes

What the proposal quietly costs them

5 min live

A yes costs something even when the money comes out of someone else's budget. It can cost control over something they own, a visible risk with their name on it, their people's time, or one more thing to keep running. Those costs rarely appear in a business case, because the person carrying them would have to admit to them. Usually the cost is a fear, not a line item, and a fear you have not named is a fear you cannot address.

LiveFour kinds of cost, none of them on the budget3 min▶

Control. Rafe built the CRM the commercial team runs on. One shared customer record means somebody else can see, and eventually change, the accounts his people own. Nobody in a meeting says "I am afraid of losing control"; they say "the data model is not ready" or "we have a major renewal season coming". Visible risk. Halvard is not against data. He is against signing a multi-year capital line whose benefits are soft, because if it disappoints, it is his signature the auditors and the board will find.

People's time. Odile's depots run on tight shifts. Any change means planners in training instead of planning, in the months her on-time number is most exposed. Operational load. Kenji is Sunniva's own boss and sponsor, and still the platform is one more thing his team must secure, patch and keep running, plus a budget argument with Rafe over who pays for the CRM side. A sponsor with an unspoken cost is a sponsor who goes quiet when it gets hard.

Self-studyReading a cost as a fear, without accusing anyone4 min▶

The objection you hear is rarely the cost underneath. "Not this year" from a director who owns the system you need usually means "I do not know what I keep". The useful move is to treat the stated objection as a clue and ask a question that lets the person name the real cost without losing face: "If this went ahead, what would you want to stay exactly as it is?"

Then change the proposal, not the person. At Fernhollow, Rafe keeps ownership of the CRM and the platform reads from it; his team keeps editing accounts where they always have. The benefit to the company is almost the same and the cost to Rafe drops from "control" to "a read connection". Separating what someone says they want from why they want it is the core of negotiation, taught in Negotiation, Positions and interests ↗; this session only needs the first half of it: name the fear before you argue the case.

Real world

Turn a slogan into a checkable claim before a CFO sees it. "People don't leave companies, they leave managers" is a slogan, and its origin is disputed. If you want to use the idea in front of someone like Halvard, find what can be checked. Gallup's own page reports that in a 2015 study of 7,272 US adults, one in two had left a job to get away from a manager (read at source on Gallup's page). That is a specific sample, a specific year and a specific question, which is exactly what a finance leader will ask for. The same discipline applies to every number in your business case: a CFO weighs a claim by its source, and a slogan has none.

Part 3 · covers the trust equation

How much do they trust you yet

5 min live

Two people can present the same proposal with the same numbers and get different answers, because the room is also deciding whether to trust the person. Maister, Green and Galford's trust equation, from The Trusted Advisor (2000), breaks that judgment into four parts: trust = (credibility + reliability + intimacy) / self-orientation. It is a practitioner model. Treat it as a checklist for where your trust is thin, not as a law that adds and divides real quantities.

LiveThe four terms, read for a new head of data3 min▶

Credibility is whether people believe what you say about your subject. Sunniva arrived with this: she was hired for it, and her first analyses were sharp. Reliability is whether you do what you said you would, repeatedly. At month three she has had no time to build a record, so it reads close to zero, however capable she is. Intimacy is whether people feel safe telling you things that are uncomfortable, the risk they have not raised in a meeting. Three months is not long enough for most executives to tell her what they are really worried about.

Self-orientation sits underneath, and it is the one most data leaders miss in themselves. When every conversation steers back to the platform, the other person hears that the conversation is about Sunniva's goal, not their problem. In the model, that one term divides all the others.

Self-studyWhat the equation is, and what it is not4 min▶

The four variable names were read on the authors' own site, trustedadvisor.com. The equation comes from a practitioner book written by consultants for consultants, and no peer-reviewed test of it was found. There is no evidence that credibility, reliability and intimacy add up as quantities, or that self-orientation divides them, and nobody has a validated scale to score any of the four. Some readers treat the arithmetic as a figure of speech; others quote it as if it were measured. Neither side has the data to settle it, so do not resolve it in your favour.

What survives the uncertainty is the checklist. Four questions, asked honestly per person: do they believe me on the subject, have I kept my promises to them, would they tell me the uncomfortable thing, and do they hear my agenda or theirs? That is useful whether or not the maths holds, and it is how the build-along uses it.

The lever a new hire can move fastest. Credibility is mostly fixed in the short run and intimacy takes time. Reliability and self-orientation can move within weeks. Make small promises and keep them visibly: "I will send you the depot numbers by Thursday", and then Thursday. And in the next three conversations, ask about their scorecard before mentioning the platform at all.

Real world

A checklist that is honest about its evidence. The Trusted Advisor (Maister, Green and Galford, 2000) is a consulting book written for professional advisers, and the equation is its central device. Its standing is the standing of a practitioner model: built from the authors' consulting experience, useful as a prompt, and untested as a formula. Saying that out loud when you use it with an executive costs you nothing and protects your credibility term, which is exactly the one a data leader cannot afford to spend.

Build-along

Write the cost ledger

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take the "must say yes" and "can say no" names from your session 1 path. You will give each of them one row: what they are measured on, what a yes costs them, the fear behind that cost, and one change to your proposal that makes it cheaper. Sunniva's rows are worked beside each step; write yours next to hers. The last column is the one that matters, because it is the only one that changes the proposal.

List the names from session 1. Everyone who must say yes and everyone who can say no. Leave out the people who are only "asked first" for now; they get a row later if they carry a cost of their own.

Write their two top metrics. Use the public signals from Part 1: the board pack, all-hands, their team's KPIs, what they bring up first. If you cannot name two, that is your first one-to-one question.

★ Step 2 · measured onMarguerite growth and margin; board confidence Halvard cost to serve; capex discipline Odile on-time delivery; depot productivity Rafe revenue and retention; key accounts Kenji uptime and security; delivery of the IT roadmap

Write what a yes costs them, in their terms. Not "budget" in general: control, visible risk, people's time or operational load, named in the words they would use.

★ Step 3 · what yes costsMarguerite another IT programme the board may ask about Halvard a multi-year spend with soft benefits Odile depot staff time during the change Rafe control of "his" CRM and account lists Kenji one more platform to run; a budget fight with Rafe

Name the fear behind the cost. Ask: if this went wrong, what would it look like for them, personally, in six months? Write it privately. This column never goes in a slide.

★ Step 4 · the fearMarguerite a programme that fails where the board can see it Halvard signing a write-off he then has to explain Odile missing a peak week with planners in training Rafe his accounts managed by a system he does not own Kenji an outage on a platform his team did not choose

Write one change to the proposal that lowers the cost. Change the plan, not the person. One change per row is enough; the best ones cost the company almost nothing.

★ Step 5 · what makes it cheaperMarguerite phase it: one depot first, decide the rest on results Halvard stage-gate the budget: release each tranche per result Odile Bram picks the first depot and sets the timing Rafe Rafe keeps CRM ownership; the platform reads from it Kenji build on the stack his team already runs; no new vendor

Score your own trust terms honestly, per person. Words, not numbers: high, some, low, none yet. Then circle the one term per person you can move before the decision, and write the smallest promise that would move it.

★ Step 6 · trust, per person credibility reliability intimacy self-orientation Halvard high none yet low high (pitched twice) Odile some low low some Rafe some none yet none high Kenji high some some some Move first: Halvard, reliability. Promise: cost-to-serve by depot, from today's three systems, by Friday.

Two ledgers, two audiences. Columns one, two and five can go into your business case: they are written in each executive's own terms. Columns three and four stay in your notebook. Writing someone's fear into a slide turns a private worry into a public accusation, and you will lose the person you were trying to reassure.

  2. Rewrite one sentence per executive. For your proposal's opening paragraph, write one version per person that names their metric first and your platform second.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Rafe says the platform should wait "until the CRM data model is ready". What is the most useful way for Sunniva to read this?

The stated objection is a clue, not necessarily the cost. Rafe's heaviest cost is control, and a change like "Rafe keeps the CRM, the platform reads from it" lowers it. A treats the clue as the whole story; B turns a cost into a fight; C ignores that Rafe can stall the platform after approval, the practical veto from session 1.

2 · Which statement about the trust equation, trust = (credibility + reliability + intimacy) / self-orientation, is accurate?

Maister, Green and Galford built the equation from consulting practice. No peer-reviewed test was found, and there is no evidence the terms add or divide. A and C claim measurement nobody has; D throws away a useful checklist. Use the four questions, and say what the model is when you use it.

3 · Sunniva is three months in. Which part of her trust can she move fastest?

Her credibility is already high, and more technical detail tends to raise self-orientation instead. Intimacy grows slowly and never in a group meeting. Reliability and self-orientation move within weeks, through small kept promises and conversations about their numbers rather than her platform. D waits two years for something she can start on Monday.
