Managing up

The first conversation on every decision path is with your own boss, and it is the one data leaders most often skip because it feels like a formality. It is not. Your boss carries your proposal into rooms you are not in, and they need things from you as much as you need things from them. Tonight we read the boss and their context, read ourselves with the same honesty, and build a working relationship that fits both people, ending with a one-page boss brief and the first message you will send.

Part 1 · covers mutual dependence

The relationship runs both ways

5 min live

The phrase "managing up" makes people wince, because it sounds like flattery or office politics. The research it comes from says the opposite. Gabarro and Kotter's Harvard Business Review article, first published in 1980 and reprinted in 2005, frames the boss relationship as mutual dependence between two fallible people, and is explicit that managing it well is not political manoeuvring or currying favour. You need your boss for resources, cover and information. Your boss needs you for results, honesty and warning. Managing up is doing your half of that on purpose.

LiveWhy your boss depends on you3 min▶

It is easy to see the dependence in one direction: Kenji controls the IT budget, so Sunniva needs him. Look at it from Kenji's chair. He is measured on uptime, security and delivering the IT roadmap. He cannot read every customer record himself, he cannot sit in the depot meetings, and he will be asked about the platform by Halvard and by Marguerite whether or not he has heard about it. He depends on Sunniva for information he cannot get, for cooperation he cannot order, and for warning before trouble reaches the executive table.

That is the point the article builds on. Both people need the other to do their own jobs well, and both are human: busy, with limits and blind spots. A relationship like that does not run itself. Someone has to look after it, and the person with less formal power usually has more to lose if nobody does.

Self-studyWhat managing up is not3 min▶

It is not flattery: telling Kenji the plan is fine when it is not makes you less useful to him, because he will repeat your view in rooms where it gets tested. It is not manipulation: steering your boss by keeping things from them, or by timing information to box them in, destroys the trust the whole relationship rests on, and it is usually discovered. And it is not doing your boss's job for them.

The professional test is simple. Would you be comfortable if your boss could read your notes about how you plan to work with them? A boss brief that says "he reads before meetings, so I will send one page the day before" passes. A note that says "I will raise it when he is too busy to object" does not. Session 5 returns to this line for influence across the whole room.

Real world

The article's own framing. Gabarro and Kotter argued that many capable managers put real effort into their teams and their peers and too little into the relationship with their own boss, often because they assume the boss will simply know what they need and say so. Their remedy is not tactics but understanding: treat the relationship as one of mutual dependence between two fallible people, and work it deliberately, for the organisation's sake as much as your own (paraphrased from the 2005 reprint, read at source).

Part 2 · covers understanding the boss and assessing yourself

Understand the boss, and yourself

5 min live

The article's checklist starts with two readings, done with the same honesty. First, the boss and their context: their goals and objectives, the pressures on them, their strengths, weaknesses and blind spots, and their preferred way of working. Second, yourself: your own strengths and weaknesses, your personal style, and your predisposition toward dependence on authority figures. Most friction between a data leader and their boss is not a disagreement about the work. It is two styles that nobody has named.

LiveReading the boss: goals, pressures, style3 min▶

Goals and pressures. Session 2 already gave Sunniva the formal version: Kenji is measured on uptime, security and the IT roadmap. The pressures are what sits behind that. He is in a running budget fight with Rafe over who owns customer systems, and every new platform is one more thing his team has to keep alive at three in the morning. A proposal that ignores either of those lands on his desk as a problem, not a gift.

Strengths, weaknesses, blind spots. Kenji is trusted by Halvard on cost and is careful about security. His blind spot, in Sunniva's reading, is that depot staff time looks free from the IT side. That matters because it is exactly what Odile will raise, and Kenji will be caught out unless Sunniva covers it.

Preferred work style. Some bosses want a document to read before they discuss anything; others want to talk it through and read later, if at all. This reader-or-listener distinction is common practice in management writing rather than the article's checklist wording, but it is the fastest style question to answer. Watch what your boss does with the last thing you sent: did they reply in writing with questions, or wait until the meeting? Kenji reads. He wants one page the day before, and he arrives with it marked up.

Self-studyAssessing yourself, including how you react to authority4 min▶

The second half of the checklist is the uncomfortable one. You cannot change your boss's style, but you can know your own and adjust for it. Sunniva's strengths are real: she has the numbers and a strong argument. Her weakness is that she is three months in, with almost no network yet. Her style is to think out loud, which works brilliantly in a workshop and badly with a boss who wants to have read it first.

The article also asks you to check your predisposition toward dependence on authority figures. In practice it tends to show up in two ways, often described as counter-dependent and over-dependent. A counter-dependent person treats the boss as an obstacle, pushes back by reflex, and quietly resents being managed; they surprise their boss because asking feels like submitting. An over-dependent person waits to be told, swallows disagreement, and lets the boss carry decisions that are theirs to make. Neither is a personality flaw to fix overnight. Both are patterns to notice, because each one damages the mutual dependence from a different side.

Sunniva reads herself as leaning counter-dependent: she came to fix the customer data, and her first instinct when Kenji hesitates is to argue rather than ask what is behind the hesitation. Writing that down is the first step to not doing it on the day.

Read from evidence, not from mood. Every line on your boss's side of the card should come from something you have seen: a decision they made, a question they asked, a document they returned. Where you have nothing, write "unknown" and make it a question for your next one-to-one. A guessed profile is worse than a gap, because you will act on it with confidence.

Part 3 · covers building a relationship that fits both

A relationship that fits both

5 min live

The third part of the checklist turns the two readings into a working relationship: one that fits both people's needs and styles, rests on expectations that have been said out loud, keeps the boss informed, is built on dependability and honesty, and uses the boss's time and resources selectively. Two habits sit on top of the article and are widely taught as common practice, not as its wording: no surprises, and bring options, not just problems.

LiveExpectations said out loud, and a boss kept informed3 min▶

Mutual expectations. Most boss friction comes from expectations neither side ever stated. Does Kenji want to approve the deck before Halvard sees it, or just be told it went? Does he want to be in the room with Rafe, or would he rather Sunniva went alone? The article's point is that you should find out what your boss expects, and make your own expectations known, rather than wait for them to emerge from a disappointment. Sunniva asks directly, in her first one-to-one about the platform, and writes the answers down.

Keep the boss informed. Kenji will be asked about the platform in corridors he walks and Sunniva does not. The flow of information he needs is more than he will ask for, and in a format he can use. For a reader, that means short written updates rather than waiting for a meeting.

Dependability and honesty. A boss who cannot rely on your dates or your numbers will check everything, which costs both of you time. Say what you will do, do it, and say early when you cannot.

Use the boss's time and resources selectively. Every favour you ask draws on a limited account. Sunniva saves Kenji's political capital for the one thing only he can do: defending the budget line against Rafe.

Self-studyNo surprises, options not problems, and where the decision tree comes from4 min▶

No surprises is common practice, not a phrase from the article, but it follows directly from mutual dependence. Your boss should never hear about your proposal, or a problem with it, from Halvard first. That is why session 1's conversation order put Kenji at 0, before Bram. A boss who is surprised in front of a peer looks uninformed, and the cost of that lands on you for months.

Bring options, not just problems is also common practice. It does not mean hiding a problem until you have solved it; that is how surprises happen. It means that when a problem needs your boss's decision, you also bring two realistic choices and your recommendation, so the conversation is about deciding rather than about worrying.

The tree in the figure is how the two habits fit together. The first question is whether the news could reach Kenji another way. If it could, timing beats completeness: tell him now, even without options. If it cannot and it needs his call, take the time to bring options. If it is inside your remit and no one else will raise it, fix it and report it at the next one-to-one. How to word the hard message itself is taught in Communication, Bad news and disagreement ↗; here the question is only who hears it first, and when.

One seam to keep clean: decision rights inside your own team, who on your team decides what, are taught in Team Management, Who decides what ↗. This session is upward: the one relationship where you hold less formal power and still carry half the responsibility.

Real world

A common pattern, not a study. An analyst finds a sharp drop in margin on one customer segment and, keen to be useful, presents it straight to the CFO in a review meeting. The CFO calls the analyst's manager that afternoon to ask why they did not mention it. The finding was right; the manager now looks uninformed in front of a peer, and the next finding from that analyst gets checked twice before it travels anywhere. The fix is cheap: a two-line note to the manager before the meeting. This is a pattern many data leaders recognise, not a measured result.

Build-along

Write the boss brief

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A boss brief is a private one-page working note, never sent to your boss, that turns the three parts of the checklist into something you can use before every one-to-one. Sunniva fills hers in for Kenji below. Write yours beside it for your own manager, from evidence where you have it and "unknown" where you do not. The last step is a real message you can send tomorrow.

Write your boss's goals. What they are measured on, from session 2, plus what they are trying to achieve this year that is not on any scorecard. Evidence only; "unknown" is allowed.

★ Step 1 · Kenji's goalsMeasured on: uptime, security, the IT roadmap delivered Trying to: show the board IT can deliver on time (said at the last town hall) Unknown: how he is judged on customer systems

Write the pressures on them. Who leans on your boss, about what, and what a bad quarter looks like from their chair.

★ Step 2 · Kenji's pressuresFrom Rafe: who owns the CRM and customer systems From Halvard: capex discipline on every new platform From his team: on-call load; one more system to keep up Bad quarter: an outage or a project that overruns

Write their style, and how they want information. Reader or listener, detail or headline, written or live, how far ahead. Base it on what they did with the last thing you sent.

★ Step 3 · Kenji's styleReader. Wants one page, the day before, not a deck. Marks it up and arrives with questions. Headline first, numbers on request. Dislikes being asked to decide in the room.

Assess yourself with the same honesty. Strengths, weaknesses, style, and how you tend to react to authority. Then name the one mismatch with your boss that will cost you most if you ignore it.

★ Step 4 · Sunniva on herselfStrengths: the numbers, a clear argument Weaknesses: three months in; thin network Style: a talker; works ideas out live Authority: leans counter-dependent: argues before asking Mismatch: he reads first, I talk first Adjust: write the page before every 1:1

Write the mutual expectations you will agree out loud. Three things you will do for your boss and two things you will ask of them. Short enough to say in one minute of a one-to-one.

★ Step 5 · to agree with KenjiI will: - tell him before anyone else hears, good or bad - send a one-page update every Thursday - bring a run plan his team can staff I ask: - the platform in next year's IT plan - his support when the CRM question reaches Rafe

Write your first "no surprises" message, in their format. Before the platform reaches anyone else on the path, your boss hears it from you, in the shape they read best. For Kenji, that is a short written note the day before you meet.

★ Step 6 · first message to KenjiKenji, before our 1:1 tomorrow, one page attached. Headline: I want to propose one shared customer record across sales, depots and billing. Two years, budget in next year's IT plan, first depot live by Q3. Why you first: nobody else has seen it. I plan to talk to Bram and Odile next, then Rafe, then Halvard. What I need from you tomorrow: whether you will sponsor it, and how you want to handle the CRM question with Rafe. Sunniva

Keep the brief, and update it. The brief is a living note. After every one-to-one, change one line: a pressure you did not know about, a style detail you got wrong. In three months it is the most accurate document you own about the person who decides most of your working life.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Finish your boss brief. All five boxes plus your self-assessment. Mark every guess as a guess, and turn each "unknown" into a question for your next one-to-one.
  2. Test the style line. Look at the last three things you sent your boss and what they did with each. Does the evidence match what you wrote in box 3?
  3. Agree one expectation out loud. In your next one-to-one, say one item from step 5 and ask your boss to add or change something. Write down what they said, in their words.
  4. Send your first "no surprises" message. Before your proposal reaches anyone else on its path, send your boss the step 6 note, in their format.

Three questions

1 · How do Gabarro and Kotter describe managing your boss?

The article frames the relationship as mutual dependence and says managing it is not manoeuvring or favour-seeking. B is the manipulation reading it rejects; C is the assumption it warns against, that the boss will simply know and say; D ignores that your boss depends on information only you have.

2 · Which of these comes from the article's checklist, rather than from common practice?

Self-assessment, including how you react to authority, is part (b) of the article's checklist. "No surprises" and "options, not problems" are widely taught common practice that fits its framing well, but they are not its words, so they should not be cited to it.

3 · A data quality problem has hit Sunniva's pilot. Halvard is reviewing the pilot numbers tomorrow and may well spot it. She has no fix yet. What should she do?

When the news could reach your boss from somewhere else, timing beats completeness: a short heads-up now, options to follow. A is right only when nobody else will raise it first; C surprises the boss in front of a peer; D fails the moment Halvard asks Kenji about it.
