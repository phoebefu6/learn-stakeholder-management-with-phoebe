# Official course map - learn-stakeholder-management-with-phoebe

Internal build document (never linked from an audience page). Phase 1 skeleton approved by Phoebe on
2026-11-06. Bucket `lead`, difficulty d2 (practitioner, no maths beyond reading a table), audience:
data and AI leads (heads of data, analytics managers, senior ICs) who must get things done through
executives and peers they do not manage. Angle: influence and politics. Single track, 6 sessions of
45 minutes, session 4 is the computed bench.

Card blurb (honour it): "Managing up and sideways: the map of who decides, what they fear, and how a
data leader earns the room."

## Evidence tiers

  or the author's own page was read on 2026-11-06. What was read is named.
- **abstract only**: the abstract or index record was read; anything beyond it is not claimed.

## Per-session coverage

### 1 Who actually decides (`01-who-actually-decides.html`)
- Formal chart vs the real decision path. Authority (the right to say yes) vs influence (the ability
  to change what the people with authority think).
- Map how a decision travels: who must say yes, who can say no (veto), who is asked first (the
  adviser), who must live with it. Distinct from RACI (link Data PMO b4) and from the power/interest
  grid (link Data PMO a5/b6): those are planning grids; this is the route a decision takes.
- Krackhardt & Hanson (1993) HBR: informal networks bypass reporting lines (HBR page summary read).
- Figure: Fernhollow's org chart with the actual decision path drawn over it.
- Build-along: draw the decision path for one real proposal of your own.
- Health line: stress content is workload and planning only, never medical advice (landing + s1).

### 2 What they are measured on, and what they fear (`02-measured-on-and-afraid-of.html`)
- Each executive's scorecard and what the proposal costs them; the cost is often a fear, not a line
  item (loss of control, a visible failure, more work for their people).
- Trust equation (Maister, Green & Galford 2000): trust = (credibility + reliability + intimacy) /
  self-orientation. Practitioner model; the four variable names read at trustedadvisor.com. No
  peer-reviewed test found: teach as a checklist, not a law.
- Seam: marketing-analytics p1 already teaches marketing/brand/BD scorecards; link it, do not reteach.
- Figure: scorecard vs cost ledger for the five executives.

### 3 Managing up (`03-managing-up.html`)
- Gabarro & Kotter, "Managing your boss", HBR Jan-Feb 1980, reprinted Jan 2005 (read at source in
  the 2005 reprint). Checklist, three parts: (a) understand the boss and their context: goals and
  objectives, pressures, strengths, weaknesses and blind spots, preferred work style; (b) assess
  yourself: strengths and weaknesses, personal style, predisposition toward dependence on authority
  figures; (c) develop and maintain a relationship that fits both people's needs and styles, has
  mutual expectations, keeps the boss informed, rests on dependability and honesty, and uses the
  boss's time and resources selectively. The article frames the relationship as mutual dependence
  and says it is not political manoeuvring.
- "No surprises" and "bring options, not problems" are common practice, not the article's wording:
  pages say "common practice".
- Seam: team-management 02 (decision rights inside one team). This session is upward.

### 4 The influence-network bench (`04-the-influence-network-bench.html`)
- Real rows: Krackhardt (1987) high-tech managers. See bench canon below.
- Betweenness and brokers: Burt (2004) AJS abstract read; Granovetter (1973) abstract read.
- Linear threshold model: Kempe, Kleinberg & Tardos (2003) KDD, read at source: each node has a
  threshold drawn uniformly from [0,1] and activates when the weighted share of active neighbours
  reaches it. The bench simplifies to ONE shared threshold set by a slider; say so.
- Teach the disagreement: threshold models are a simplification; advice vs friendship ties give
  different maps (Krackhardt's own point about the friendship network, reported via the 1992 chapter).

### 5 Coalitions and pre-wiring (`05-coalitions-and-pre-wiring.html`)
- Sequencing one-to-ones before the room; nobody should hear the proposal for the first time in the
  meeting where it is decided. Common practice.
- Cohen & Bradford, Influence Without Authority (3rd ed., Wiley 2017): reported. Six-step model
  reported via the publisher's press release: assume everyone is a potential ally; clarify your
  goals and priorities; diagnose the other person's world; identify relevant currencies; deal with
  relationships; influence through give and take. The five currency families (inspiration, task,
  position, relationship, personal) are reported from summaries, not read in the book.
- Cialdini, Influence (new and expanded edition 2021): seven principles named on influenceatwork.com
  (reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity, unity).
  Replication caution: Gamian-Wilk & Dolinski (2019) Psychological Reports directly replicated the
  foot-in-the-door study (the commitment technique) and found no significant effect in Poland or
  Ukraine; their abstract says meta-analytic effects are small. Do not generalise to all seven.
- Ethics line, a professional norm: influence states its real interest and survives being said out
  loud; manipulation depends on the other person not knowing what you are doing.
- Seam: negotiation course (interests vs positions, BATNA, trades). Link from Part 2.

### 6 Losing a stakeholder, and the long game (`06-losing-a-stakeholder.html`)
- Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004) J. Applied Psychology 89(1):104-118 (abstract read): trust was
  repaired more when the person apologised for a competence-based violation and denied culpability
  for an integrity-based one; an apology repaired more when later evidence showed guilt, a denial
  more when later evidence showed innocence. Method reported (WashU press release): business
  students acting as hirers, a candidate accused of a tax-return error. Participant counts not read.
  Teach the nuance: for a data leader who shipped a wrong number, the violation is usually
  competence-based and the honest repair is an apology with a fix. Never deny what you did.
- Capstone: one-page stakeholder operating plan.
- Seam: communication 06 (delivering bad news) is the message; this session is the relationship after.

## Bench canon (session 4) - computed 2026-11-06, python == node on 10,010 values

Data: Krackhardt, D. (1987). Cognitive social structures. Social Networks 9(2):109-134,
DOI 10.1016/0378-8733(87)90009-8 (abstract read via OpenAlex: "a management team of a small
manufacturing firm"). 21 managers; advice, friendship and reports-to ties, plus age, tenure, level,
department. Firm details reported by the UCINET dataset page (high-tech equipment, US west coast,
just over 100 employees). Rows as shipped in CRAN NetData 0.3 (GPL-2), file data/kracknets.rda, read
from https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cran/NetData/master/data/kracknets.rda (archive:
https://cran.r-project.org/src/contrib/Archive/NetData/NetData_0.3.tar.gz). Builder asserts 190
advice, 102 friendship, 20 reports-to arcs (the published counts). NetData's help page describes a
different Krackhardt study (Silicon Systems, a union campaign); the rows it ships are the 21-manager
data. Pages cite Krackhardt (1987) for the data and NetData for the copy.

Formal structure: manager 7 is the CEO (level 1); 2, 14, 18, 21 are vice presidents (level 2).
Team sizes: VP 14 has 7 reports, VP 21 has 4, VP 2 has 3, VP 18 has 2.

Model: a manager backs the proposal once at least TH percent of the people they go to for advice
(or name as friends) back it; one shared TH; synchronous rounds until nothing changes. Decision rule:
11 or more of 21 back it AND the CEO (7) backs it. Random: 200 seeded draws (mulberry32, seed
20261106). Orders: org = level, then team size, then id; in-degree desc then id; betweenness desc then
id (unnormalised Brandes, directed).

### Measured (advice ties)
- (modelled) Rounds to settle at the default: org chart 2, most-asked 1, brokers 6. Org chart backers: 2, 6, 7, 11, 12, 14, 21.
- In-degree (who is asked): 2 = 18 (a VP), 18 = 15, 21 = 15, 1 = 13 (a level-3 manager), 7 = 13 (CEO),
  11 = 11. The most-asked person is VP 2, not the CEO; a manager ties the CEO.
- Betweenness: 18 = 88.9, 21 = 60.1, 7 = 27.6, 10 = 18.3 (level-3 manager), 1 = 13.7 and 4 = 13.7 (as the widget prints them). VP 14, who runs
  the biggest team (7 reports), is asked by 10 and has betweenness 0.6.
- Manager 6 goes to only one person for advice (VP 21).
- Broker leaves (18): 0 pairs lose every path; 43 ordered pairs need a longer route.
  (If 21 left instead, manager 6 would be cut off from 19 colleagues: not a widget output, do not quote.)

### Measured (friendship ties)
- Most named as a friend: 2 = 10, 1 = 8, 12 = 8. Top betweenness: 17 = 134.4, 11 = 58.5, 21 = 33.9.
- The CEO (7) and manager 9 name no friends. On friendship ties the CEO can back the proposal only if
  he is pre-wired personally.
- Broker leaves (17): 113 ordered pairs lose every path; 9 need a longer route.

### Modelled ladder, advice ties (supporters of 21; rule = passes/fails)
Default settings: advice ties, threshold 65%, pre-wire 3.
| Strategy | People | Backers | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top of the org chart (anti-lever) | 7, 14, 21 | 7 | fails |
| Most-asked | 2, 18, 21 | 5 | fails |
| Brokers | 18, 21, 7 | 21 | passes |
| Random, 200 draws | any 3 | 3.5 avg (exact 3.55; the widget prints 3.5) | 1 of 200 |

Break, the broker (18) leaves, same settings:
- Brokers, plan as it stood (21, 7): 21 of 21 -> 9 of 20, rule fails. Re-mapped on the 20 who remain
  (21, 10, 4: the CEO drops out of the top three once 18 is gone): 20 of 20, rule passes.
- Most-asked as it stood (2, 21): 4 of 20; re-mapped (2, 21, 1): 20 of 20.
- Org chart as it stood (7, 14, 21): 9 of 20 (higher than 7, because 18 was a non-backer who no
  longer counts); re-mapped 9.
- Random: 4.5 avg of 20 (exact 4.48), passes in 12 of 200.

Other settings a page may quote (advice ties):
- Threshold 60, pre-wire 2: org (7, 14) 2; most-asked (2, 18) 2; brokers (18, 21) 21, passes.
- Threshold 30, pre-wire 2 or more: every strategy reaches 21. When everyone moves easily, who you
  pre-wire stops mattering.
- Threshold 80, pre-wire 3: org 6, most-asked 4, brokers 7. When everyone is hard to move, three
  conversations are not enough for anyone.
- Grid-wide counts below come from stk_reference.py over the whole grid; the widget does NOT print
  them, so no page may quote them. Across all 55 settings (thresholds 30-80 by 5, pre-wire 1-5): brokers beat the org chart in 21,
  tie in 29, lose in 5. All 5 losses are pre-wire 1 at thresholds 30-50 (the CEO alone against
  VP 18 alone). The rule passes in 36 settings for brokers, 30 for most-asked, 24 for the org chart.

### Modelled ladder, friendship ties (default 65%, pre-wire 3)
- Org chart (7, 14, 21): 3, fails. Most-named (2, 1, 12): 8, fails. Brokers (17, 11, 21): 3, fails.
  Random 3.4 avg (exact 3.415), 0 of 200.
- (Not widget outputs; never quote.) Across 55 settings the rule passes for the org chart in 7, never for most-named or brokers, because
  only a plan that pre-wires the CEO personally can include him. Most-named beats brokers in 44 of 55.
- Teaching point: the two maps disagree. Advice ties decide whether a proposal spreads; friendship ties
  tell you who listens to whom off the record. Krackhardt's own argument was that the friendship
  network matters for anticipating resistance (reported).

- Friendship 2, 7, 17: 6 of 21, fails.

## Famous claims: tiered or cut

- "People don't leave companies, they leave managers": reported slogan, origin disputed. Use instead
  Gallup's page (read at source): in a 2015 study of 7,272 US adults, one in two had left a job to get
  away from a manager. https://www.gallup.com/workplace/236570/employees-leave-managers.aspx
- "70% of change programmes fail": Hughes (2011) Journal of Change Management 11(4):451-464 (abstract
  read) reviewed five published instances of the claim and found no valid, reliable empirical
  evidence for it. Teach the disagreement if mentioned; never quote 70% as fact.
- "Influence is X% of success" statistics: none found at source; cut.
- Mendelow's power/interest grid: commonly cited as Mendelow (1991) "Stakeholder mapping", 2nd ICIS,
  Cambridge MA. No such paper could be found; the verifiable paper is Mendelow (1981), ICIS
  Proceedings paper 20, whose abstract does not mention a grid. Pages say "commonly attributed to
  Mendelow" and link to Data PMO; never cite 1991.
- Pfeffer, Managing with Power (HBS Press 1992): reported (publisher blurb): implementation is the
  core challenge, and it is about influencing behaviour and overcoming resistance.

## Not covered (honest list)

- Formal stakeholder-analysis grids, RACI, comms plans and steering cadence (Data PMO owns them).
- Running the meeting (Facilitation), the bad-news message (Communication), deal-making mechanics
  (Negotiation), decision rights inside a team (Team Management).
- Organisational network analysis surveys at scale (ONA tools, passive email metadata): privacy and
  consent issues deserve their own treatment.
- Stress and wellbeing beyond workload and planning: never medical or clinical advice.
- Cross-cultural differences in managing up and hierarchy: real, under-evidenced here, not taught.
